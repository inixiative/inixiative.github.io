chapter 03 · the signal

The signal, not the prescription

If governance is a search, someone has to define what "better" means — or the search optimizes noise. Here is the deepest design decision in this proposal: citizens define the destination, not the route. The public's role is not to write policy, adjudicate mechanisms, or micromanage administration. It is to continuously broadcast the shape of the society they want to live in — and to judge, from lived experience, whether they're getting it. Specialists then compete to propose mechanisms that move toward that shape, and are paid, retained, or sunset strictly by whether the signal says it's working.

The whitepaper compresses the division of labor into one line, borrowed from the courtroom: expertise does the work; the public determines whether the work was done well. A jury doesn't perform the surgery, doesn't write the contract, doesn't run the audit — it renders the one verdict no specialist may render on themselves: was this right? This proposal applies the jury principle to the whole of government.

measuring the sensors

What does the signal actually measure? Not GDP, not test scores, not any index a statistician can compose — those are proxies, and every proxy is a future Goodhart casualty. The signal is the population's sensor reads: Do you feel society is fair? Do consequences fall equally on elites and commoners? Is everyone accountable to the same law — are the courts honest? Do you feel you have opportunity — that effort and ability are rewarded? Do you have control over your own life? Are you optimistic about the future? These sound soft. They are the opposite. As the whitepaper puts it: you can fake a GDP number, a crime statistic, a test score — you cannot fake the feeling of opportunity or fairness across millions of people. The human sensor network is the ground truth that metrics try, and fail, to proxy. GDP can rise while sensed fairness collapses; when they diverge, it is the metric that is lying.

This is why the felt questions are the terminal values of the whole design. They are not a stand-in for the thing we want; they are the thing we want. A society where people feel treated fairly, rewarded for effort, and hopeful about the future is the goal — there is no further fact behind it that the questions are approximating. You cannot optimize "people feel the system is fair" without actually making the system fairer; the measurement and the condition measured are the same thing.

no metric may override the verdict

The corollary has teeth, and the whitepaper applies them twice. First: leaders' long-term pay is bound only to the broad did-this-work signal — never to named KPIs — because any named metric that binds compensation is a Goodhart target, and a leader who nominates their own KPI will nominate a hittable proxy. Second: no leader may ever cite a metric to override public judgment. "The graphs say it's working" is not a defense against a population that says it isn't. If a leader genuinely believes a policy follows a J-curve — pain now, payoff later — the honest instrument exists: declare the pain window and a vindication test up front, and stake on it. Crying "trust the metric" is thereby made expensive when false and cheap when true, without any metric ever overriding the broad signal. Quantitative data still matters — as color, context, and diagnosis. Metrics are investigative tools. Sentiment is the governance signal.

the signal is also the belonging

One more thing rides on this design, easy to miss. Coordination systems historically smuggle their binding energy in through myth and hierarchy — the flag, the throne, the founding story. This design makes the bet explicit: participation in the monitoring is the belonging. You feel part of the society because your signal demonstrably moves it — your sensor read is part of the verdict that pays, retains, or retires every law and every leader. That is an empirical bet that legibility can substitute for myth, and it is why chapter 13 treats the integrity of the signal's formation — the epistemic environment — as the load-bearing wall of the entire system.
