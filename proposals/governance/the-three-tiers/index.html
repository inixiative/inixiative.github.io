chapter 04 · the architecture

Propose · decide · kill

Classical separation of powers splits government by branch — legislative, executive, judicial — and then watches the branches collude, because each branch holds a complete power loop of its own. This proposal splits by function instead, so that no tier can act alone: one tier can only propose, one can only decide, and one can only judge and remove. Each is incomplete without the others.

Leaders propose. The people we now call politicians hold exactly one legislative power: drafting well-constructed policy and spending a scarce proposal budget on it (chapter 5). They cannot pass anything. Their craft is design; their reward is durability pay (chapter 7).

Delegates decide. Passage is voted by citizens' chosen delegates — individuals who have earned enough delegated trust to cross the participation threshold at a given scale. Delegation is granular, decaying, and instantly revocable; weight held is weight continuously re-earned. Delegates are what a bureaucracy was supposed to be — attention on behalf of those who cannot attend — but revocable in real time. They draw a stipend while they serve and carry real standing in society — but no long tail: unlike leaders, they build no portfolio of dividends. Their entire payoff is tenure, and tenure is delegated support — support that decays, that can be withdrawn in a day, and that citizens can actively vote against. A delegate's long game is simply to keep deserving the weight. And the pool itself renews by a rule that is easy to state: entry by lot, tenure by merit. Each cycle, delegates who have kept their delegators' support remain in the pool — good attention persists. Delegates who fall out of favor drop out, and their vacated seats are filled by random draw from the eligible citizenry — not by campaigning. There is no ladder to climb into the deciding tier, and no way to lobby your way back: a delegate who loses the room rejoins the lottery like everyone else. The pool is always the surviving incumbents plus a fresh random cohort — continuity for the trusted, a door that opens by chance rather than ambition, and an exit that stays shut behind whoever the delegators pushed out. (Cohort size, support thresholds, and draw eligibility are parameters; chapter 14 covers what the randomness buys.)

Citizens judge and remove. The broadest tier holds everything terminal: open agenda-setting — anyone may raise a question to the forum and vote on whether it matters, costless in points, because mapping what society cares about is the base democratic task — the ongoing verdict on every standing law (chapter 8), removal of leaders who lose legitimacy, and exclusive custody of the constitutional layer (chapter 12).

Citizens can kill but not draft; delegates can decide but not propose; leaders can propose but not decide. Rome stumbled onto a fragment of this insight once — the tribunes, a pure veto held by the broadest class — and it worked until it was assassinated. This design generalizes the tribunician insight into the whole architecture.

constitutive, not consultative

The bottom band of that diagram is the load-bearing one. The rules for how the upper tiers are entered and weighted — how delegation is earned, what qualifications matter and how much — belong directly and exclusively to the broadest tier. In the whitepaper's formulation: smaller, specialized cohorts operate within rules they did not write and cannot rewrite, and the broadest cohort's authority over those rules is constitutive, not consultative. Ordinary policy authority may be delegated away; the structure of the delegation itself may not be. That is the answer this design gives to the oldest question in political theory — quis custodiet ipsos custodes? Custody of the custodian-rules is held by everyone. Chapter 12 covers how that layer is made slow enough to be safe.
