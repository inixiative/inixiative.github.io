Voting that captures intent

Chapter 1 called the modern ballot a one-bit signal. Here is what a wider channel looks like — and why the width is not a luxury but the difference between a system that hears its citizens and one that merely counts them.

the parable of the five voters

Five people face a proposal, A. Two of them like it. Three of them strongly oppose it — but they're split on what they'd prefer instead. Under binary voting, A wins: two votes for, and the opposition's intensity counts for nothing because a vote has no magnitude. The outcome is exactly backwards from what the group actually wants — a minority's mild preference defeats a majority's strong objection, because the channel couldn't carry the word strong.

Now give each voter a budget of points, spendable for or against anything on the docket. The two supporters put +1, +1 on A; they like it, but not enough to spend more. The three opponents put −2, −2, −2 — they don't need to agree on an alternative to register that this matters to them. Net: −4. A loses. Nothing exotic happened; the system simply translated political will correctly, because the signal carried three dimensions instead of one: engagement (did you spend attention on this at all), direction (for or against), and magnitude (how much of your scarce budget it was worth to you).

what the three dimensions buy

Each citizen receives a modest point budget per cycle. The budget forces prioritization — you cannot scream maximum intensity at everything — which means every allocation is information. Indifference finally shows up in the data: proposals that fail to draw engagement simply don't pass, no matter how their small audience leans, which is the filter that keeps a passionate clique from legislating for an indifferent public. And priority emerges by consent: the questions that gather the most backing are, definitionally, the ones the public most wants worked on. Taiwan's digital democracy experiments have been circling exactly this insight — surface what has broad support behind it, rather than whatever wedge issue splits 51/49. A bare-majority win on a low-engagement question is not a mandate; it's an artifact of the old channel.

Quadratic voting — Weyl and Posner's scheme where a vote's cost grows quadratically — deserves its nod here: it is the best-known academic answer to intensity capture, and convex cost curves of that family can serve as an optional damper within point-voting. This design deliberately doesn't adopt it wholesale. Its efficient-market framing prizes fast preference-response, and speed is not a virtue in governance — deliberated, sustained judgment is. The point budget plus engagement floors capture intensity without importing the market metaphor.

windows, not moments

Voting here is not an event; it is a window. Anything entering a voting cycle must have been submitted the cycle before — a built-in deliberation lag that kills midnight riders and surprise votes. Points are allocatable and freely movable throughout the window, locking only at the boundary, so early voters are never punished by information that emerges mid-window, and a vote cast five minutes before the boundary weighs the same as one cast five days before. Batching decisions to a shared cadence — the governance tickrate — is what keeps a fast digital system deliberate at human speed: accumulate during the window, resolve at the boundary, and never let "who can sustain attention longest" substitute for "what has genuine support."

passage: engagement plus approval

Passing requires clearing both floors: enough engagement to prove the question matters, and net approval among the weight engaged. The engagement floor counts affirmative weight — a required share of eligible weight actually in favor — never mere turnout. The distinction sounds technical and is anything but: participation quorums teach opponents to stay home, because absence kills quorum more reliably than a no-vote (Italy's referendum system died of exactly this). Silence must never count as a vote. Delegated weight counts toward engagement, which is what protects the boring-but-vital — pension solvency, grid maintenance — from starving in an attention-gated system: delegates exist precisely to supply professional attention where public attention is thin (chapter 4).

Bundles get one honest concession: items may pass together as a negotiated package when their budgets only reconcile jointly — but they decouple legally the moment they pass. Severability is the default; each atom thereafter lives or dies on its own record. Omnibus-forever is how attribution dies (chapter 14).

open decision Tally visibility during the window. Live tallies produce herding and deadline sniping; sealed tallies starve deliberation of feedback. The candidate middle: engagement levels public — so citizens can see what is contested and worth scarce points — with direction sealed until lock.
